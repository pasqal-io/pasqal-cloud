Effective noise channels

Introduction

Section titled “Introduction”

Effective noise channels were introduced in the introductory tutorial Simulating sequences with errors and noises. They will be further detailed in this notebook. We recommend you to check the introductory tutorial before going through this one.

The effective noise channels implemented here are based on the concept of collapse operators which are actors in the Lindblad equation. If you’re not familiar with Lindblad equations or want to go further, please check the resource here (external).

Recall the Lindblad equation:

dρdt=−i[H,ρ]+∑k>0(LkρLk†−12{Lk†Lk,ρ})\frac{d \rho}{dt} = -i[\mathcal{H}, \rho] + \sum_{k>0} \left( L_k \rho L_k^\dagger - \frac{1}{2} \left\{ L_k^\dagger L_k, \rho \right\} \right)dtdρ​=−i[H,ρ]+k>0∑​(Lk​ρLk†​−21​{Lk†​Lk​,ρ})

The operators LkL_kLk​ are often called collapse operators. Each set of LkL_kLk​ gives birth to a different effective noise channel. We will discuss in this tutorial two specific ones, whose expressions are developed in this resource (see chapter 8.3) (external):

  • The dephasing channel

  • The depolarizing channel

The dephasing channel

Section titled “The dephasing channel”

Dephasing channel models noises that modify the system into a mixture of states such that the phase cannot be accurately predicted.

The dephasing noise can be thought of as arising from random additional phases being added on some components of the state at a rate γph,state\gamma_{ph, state}γph,state​. For instance, if a system is addressed by a Rydberg and a Raman channel, it is described by three states (∣r>,∣g>,∣h>\left |r\right >, \left |g\right >, \left |h\right >∣r⟩,∣g⟩,∣h⟩) and the associated collapse operators are:

L1=2γph,r∣r><r∣,L2=2γph,h∣h><h∣\begin{split}L_1 = \sqrt{2\gamma_{ph, r}} \left|r \right> \left <r \right |, \\ L_2 = \sqrt{2\gamma_{ph, h}} \left|h \right> \left <h \right |\end{split}L1​=2γph,r​​∣r⟩⟨r∣,L2​=2γph,h​​∣h⟩⟨h∣​

The depolarizing channel

Section titled “The depolarizing channel”

Under the depolarizing noise, a system in any state evolves to the maximally mixed state at a rate γpol\gamma_{pol}γpol​. This noise channel can only describe 2-level systems (system addressed by one channel). The corresponding collapse operators are:

L1=γpol4σx,L2=γpol4σy,L3=γpol4σz\begin{split}L_1 = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma_{pol}}{4}} \sigma_x, \\ L_2 = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma_{pol}}{4}}\sigma_y, \\ L_3 = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma_{pol}}{4}} \sigma_z\end{split}L1​=4γpol​​​σx​,L2​=4γpol​​​σy​,L3​=4γpol​​​σz​​

Bloch sphere representation

Section titled “Bloch sphere representation”

To better understand the effect of each channel on a single-qubit density matrix, let us represent it on the Bloch sphere.

Any density matrix of a mixed state may be written as ρ=I+r⃗.σ⃗2\rho = \frac{\mathbb{I} + \vec{r}.\vec{\sigma}}{2}ρ=2I+r.σ​, where r⃗\vec{r}r is the vector representing the mixed state on the Bloch sphere.

The effect of a channel on a density matrix is modeled using Kraus operators, such that ρ(t)=∑iMiρ(0)Mi†\rho(t) = \sum_i M_i \rho(0) M^\dagger_iρ(t)=∑i​Mi​ρ(0)Mi†​. These Kraus operators MiM_iMi​ are:

  • for the dephasing channel: M0=1−pph2  IM_0 = \sqrt{1- \frac{p_{ph}}{2}} \,\, \mathbb{I}M0​=1−2pph​​​I, M1=pph2  σzM_1 = \sqrt{\frac{p_{ph}}{2}} \,\, \sigma_zM1​=2pph​​​σz​.

  • for the depolarizing channel: M0=1−3ppol4  IM_0 = \sqrt{1- \frac{3p_{pol}}{4}} \,\, \mathbb{I}M0​=1−43ppol​​​I, M1=ppol4σxM_1 = \sqrt{\frac{p_{pol}}{4}} \sigma_xM1​=4ppol​​​σx​, M2=ppol4σyM_2 = \sqrt{\frac{p_{pol}}{4}} \sigma_yM2​=4ppol​​​σy​ and M3=ppol4σzM_3 = \sqrt{\frac{p_{pol}}{4}} \sigma_zM3​=4ppol​​​σz​

It can be noted that the Kraus operators fulfill the completion relation ∑iMi=I\sum_{i} M_i = \mathbb{I}∑i​Mi​=I, and that they use the probability over the whole experiment ppp instead of the rate γ\gammaγ used in collapse operators. The link between Kraus operators and collapse operators is developed in the first reference (external).

Depolarizing channel

Section titled “Depolarizing channel”

The depolarizing channel contracts the Bloch sphere across all directions. We could try to come up with an inflation channel that would revert the state’s depolarization. However, such a channel would not be positive and thus not a valid superoperator, confirming that the coherence lost cannot be recovered.

Dephasing Channel

Section titled “Dephasing Channel”

In the following we illustrate the results of the single qubit dephasing channel.

This plot shows the Rabi oscillations over time. You can see the effects of the dephasing channel on the populations of the excited and ground states. When we increase the noise of the dephasing channel, the Rabi oscillations are increasingly damped.

The dephasing channel destroys off-diagonal elements of the density matrix, and tends towards a stationary state with zero off-diagonals. The mixed state obtained is the maximally mixed state.

In other words the density matrix converges to ρ=12I=12(∣0⟩⟨0∣+∣1⟩⟨1∣)\rho = \frac{1}{2} \mathbb I = \frac{1}{2}(|0\rangle\langle 0| + |1\rangle\langle 1|)ρ=21​I=21​(∣0⟩⟨0∣+∣1⟩⟨1∣).

Notice that the ground state and excited state populations oscillate in phase opposition.

Depolarizing Channel

Section titled “Depolarizing Channel”

In the following we illustrate the results of the single qubit depolarizing channel.

As was the case for the dephasing channel, we observe a damping of the population of both basis states, indicating a convergence torwards the maximally mixed state. However, for matching rate values, the depolarizing channel results in a faster decoherence than the dephasing channel.

If we recall the effect of both channels on the Bloch sphere, we see that the depolarizing channel acts uniformly for any state, while the dephasing channel’s effect is maximized for Bloch vectors lying on the equator. Therefore, as the Bloch vector oscillates between ∣0⟩\ket{0}∣0⟩ and ∣1⟩\ket{1}∣1⟩, it still gets progressively smaller. However, its decoherence rate only matches that of the depolarizing channel when crossing the equator, resulting in a compartively longer coherence time.

Two-atom noisy simulations

Section titled “Two-atom noisy simulations”

We already know what happens in a single qubit configuration, but we would like to study the multi-qubit case. To do so we start with two qubits.

We will analyze two configurations. One with nearby atoms and another with distant atoms and see how effective noise channels interact with the system.

The concepts of far and nearby are defined with respect to the Rydberg blockade radius. Two atoms are nearby when they are within a blockade radius of one another and far otherwise.

Depolarizing Channel

Section titled “Depolarizing Channel”

In the following we illustrate the results of the two-qubit depolarizing channel.

Although the Rydberg blockade should make the state ∣rr⟩\ket{rr}∣rr⟩ state unreacheble, we observe its population gradually increase over time. This is explained by the fact that the depolarizing noise is making each state vector evolve independently towards the maximally mixed state. Therefore, as coherence is lost, the ∣rr⟩\ket{rr}∣rr⟩ becomes just as likely as any other eigenstate, since both qubits turn into classical mixtures with no correlation between them.

Thus, the final output mixed state is:

ρ=14(∣gg⟩⟨gg∣+∣gr⟩⟨gr∣+∣rg⟩⟨rg∣+∣rr⟩⟨rr∣)\rho = \frac{1}{4} (\ket{gg}\bra{gg} + \ket{gr}\bra{gr} + \ket{rg}\bra{rg} + \ket{rr}\bra{rr})ρ=41​(∣gg⟩⟨gg∣+∣gr⟩⟨gr∣+∣rg⟩⟨rg∣+∣rr⟩⟨rr∣)
